# Reciprocity and Relationship Maintenance

Use this reference when the user asks whether a non-romantic relationship is
one-sided, how much to invest, how to maintain a professional or social
connection, whether to keep helping, or how to respond to repeated unmet
commitments.

## Assess events, not personal worth

Do not score a person, rank “relationship value,” or treat reciprocity as an
immediate exchange of equal favors.

Review a meaningful time window using:

- **reliability:** commitments are kept or risks are raised early;
- **responsiveness:** important messages or needs receive a proportionate
  response;
- **initiative:** connection and coordination do not always depend on one
  person;
- **burden:** time, money, emotional labor, access, and risk are not
  chronically displaced onto one side without agreement;
- **repair:** misses are acknowledged and corrected;
- **respect for limits:** a refusal does not trigger pressure or punishment;
- **mutual benefit:** each person can name some legitimate value in the
  relationship, even when contributions differ;
- **renegotiability:** changing capacity or needs can be discussed.

Treat temporary asymmetry differently from an established pattern. Caregiving,
crisis, onboarding, sponsorship, illness, and unequal formal roles can create
legitimate asymmetry. The question is whether the arrangement is understood,
voluntary where choice exists, sustainable, and revisable.

## Use the Event–Pattern–Agreement method

1. **Event:** What happened, when, and with what impact?
2. **Pattern:** Is it isolated, situational, or repeated across comparable
   events?
3. **Existing agreement:** What was explicitly or reasonably expected?
4. **Context:** What constraint is known rather than inferred?
5. **Renegotiation:** What future arrangement would be sustainable?
6. **Test:** What observable behavior during what window would show change?
7. **Decision:** Maintain, narrow, pause, formalize, or exit the dependency.

Output:

> One event does not establish a one-sided relationship. The stronger pattern
> is ___. The current arrangement expects ___. A sustainable revision is ___.
> Reassess after ___; if ___ continues, reduce ___.

## Distinguish generosity from hidden contracts

Before giving help, ask:

- Am I willing to do this if no equivalent favor returns?
- Have I stated any real limit or condition?
- Is the recipient able to decline?
- Does this displace a commitment or create resentment?
- Am I hoping the favor will purchase access, affection, loyalty, silence, or
  promotion?

If an exchange condition matters, make it explicit and ethical. Do not give
freely in words and invoice the person emotionally later.

## Maintain professional relationships

Use light, specific, non-extractive contact:

1. remember the person's work, interests, or stated priorities without building
   a private dossier;
2. share information or recognition when genuinely relevant;
3. make bounded requests with easy decline;
4. close the loop after advice or an introduction;
5. credit help accurately;
6. reconnect with a purpose rather than fake urgency.

Starter:

> Your advice on ___ helped me change ___. The result was ___. Thank you. I
> also saw ___ and thought it might be relevant to your work; no response
> needed.

Do not manufacture constant contact. Some healthy professional relationships
are low-frequency and context-specific.

## Handle repeated helping and rescue work

When the user repeatedly rescues another person:

- identify the immediate problem the rescue solves;
- identify the system or behavior the rescue allows to continue;
- calculate the displaced work and exposure;
- decide whether help is emergency, teaching, shared work, or ownership
  transfer;
- set scope, owner, and end condition before helping again.

Starter:

> I can help recover this handoff once today. I cannot remain the fallback
> owner. Before the next deadline, we need the named owner, warning point, and
> escalation route.

Helping becomes enabling when it repeatedly removes the consequence or
feedback needed for the real owner or system to change.

## Repair uneven investment

Use:

1. a neutral description of the pattern;
2. the practical or relational impact;
3. curiosity about a real constraint;
4. a narrower, reciprocal agreement;
5. an observation window;
6. willingness to reduce dependency if the agreement fails.

Avoid proving that the other person “cares.” Decide whether the observable
arrangement works.

## Manage triangulation

Triangulation occurs when a person recruits a third party to carry a message,
validate a private theory, or pressure someone instead of using an appropriate
direct or formal route.

Before involving a third party, state the legitimate function:

- decision authority;
- missing information;
- mediation;
- representation or protection;
- verification;
- formal review.

Do not use allies for gossip, reputation warfare, or manufactured consensus.
In unsafe or high-power cases, indirect supported communication can be
appropriate; it should still have a clear function and factual boundary.

## Reduce or exit investment

Consider narrowing or ending the dependency when:

- important commitments repeatedly fail without warning or repair;
- boundaries trigger pressure, retaliation, or ridicule;
- the arrangement requires concealment or unethical conduct;
- the user bears chronic cost without a viable renegotiation;
- the relationship blocks safer or more sustainable alternatives;
- the observation window has ended without the agreed change.

Exit language should be proportionate:

> This arrangement is no longer workable for me. I will complete ___ by ___,
> then I will no longer own ___. Future requests need to go through ___.

Do not use withdrawal as a secret test designed to provoke pursuit.

## Failure modes

- immediate tit-for-tat accounting;
- numerical relationship or influence scores;
- interpreting different contribution types as no contribution;
- hiding a contract inside generosity;
- endless patience without an observation window;
- using empathy to erase opportunity cost;
- manufacturing contact to seem close;
- treating networking as extraction;
- recruiting allies to punish or isolate someone;
- reducing investment without communicating a material ownership change.

## Evidence boundary

Social-exchange and reciprocity theories are influential but conceptually
varied. They support examining contingent exchange and mutual obligation; they
do not validate a personal scorecard or predict one person's loyalty.
Event-based observation, explicit agreement, and review are product safeguards
created for this skill. See `EXCHANGE-01` in
[the evidence ledger](evidence-ledger.md).
